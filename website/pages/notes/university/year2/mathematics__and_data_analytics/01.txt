-- Sequences & Series

Throughout, $n$ is the index of a sequence element ($n=0$ is the index of the initial element)
A summation of a sequence is called a series, and is the addition of all elements from $n=0$ up to and including $n=n$

An arithmetic sequence is one where each subsequent element has a common added difference, e.g. $1,2.5,4,4.5\dots$
Arithmetic sequence : $a_n=a_0+d(n-1)$ where a_0 is the initial element and $d$ is the difference
Arithmetic series : $S_n=\frac 12n(2a_0+d(n-1))$, or also $S_n=\frac 12n(a_0+a_n)$
The example can be defined as $a_n=1+1.5(n-1)$

A geometric sequence is one where each subsequent element has a common multiplied difference, e.g. $1,0.5,0.25,0.125\dots$
Geometric sequence : $a_n=a_0r^{n-1}$ where $r$ is the multiplier
Geometric series : $S_n=a\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}$
If $|r|<1$, as $\n\rightarrow\infty$ then $S_\infty=\frac a{1-r}$ (sum to infinity of sequence)
The sum to infinity of the previous example is $\frac 1{1-0.5}=2$

For summations, $r$ is used as the incremented variable and $n$ is used as the maximum value of the variable (both are integers and the increment is 1)
With summation notation, the start is put below the symbol and the end is put above (can also be top-right and bottom-right)
Additions within a summation can be separated, e.g. $\sum^n_{r=1}r+r^2=\sum^n_{r=1}r+\sum^n_{r=1}$
Any coefficient / variable independent of the incrementation can also be factorised out, e.g. $\sum^n_{r=1}3x+2yr=3x\sum^n_{r=1}1+2y\sum^n_{r=1}r$
For summations that start with $r>1$, split it into a subtraction of the sum 1 to $r$ from the sum 1 to $n$

The sum of integers : $\sum^n_{r=1}1=n$
The sum of $r$ : $\sum^n_{r=1}r=\frac 12n(n+1)$
The sum of $r^2$ : $\sum^n_{r=1}r^2=\frac 16n(n+1)(2n+1)$
The sum of $r^3$ : $\sum^n_{r=1}r^3=\frac 14n^2(n+1)^2$
When finding the sum of a factorised equation, expand it into a polynomial and split it into separate summations

When finding the sum of polynomial denominators, split it into partial fractions and solve separately
Method of differences is where you list the partial fractions for each case, then see which terms cancel out between cases
You do this with the first few cases (until you find the pattern) then with the last few cases, then the series is the summation of these terms
Partial fractions are bottom heavy so will always converge {talk about separating top heavy}
The limit is the constant term of the series
To show limits, factorise top-heavy fraction or divide numerator and denominator by highest polynomial term

Ratio test is for series $\sum^\infty_{r=0}a_r$ where $a_r\geq0\forall r$
For this, the limit $L$ of the ratio of consecutive terms is calculated : $\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{a_{r+1}}{a_r}
If $L<1$, the series is convergent; if $L>1$, the series is divergent; and if $L=1$, the test is inconclusive

Infinite things be goofy, so some series, particularly those with negative and positive terms, can be re-arranged to be either convergent or divergent, so are only conditionaly convergent as opposed to absolutely convergent
Because of this, an absolutely converging series is defined as $\sum^\infty_{r=0}|a_r|=L$

Polynomials can be used to approximate other curves, an example being Maclaurin polynomials
Maclaurin polynomials $p(x)$ are found using $p(x)=f(0)+\frac{f'(0)}{1!}x+\frac{f''(0)}{2!}x^2+\frac{f'''(0)}{3!}x^3+\dots$ or $p(x)=\sum^n_{r=0}\frac{f^{(r)}(0)}{r!}x^r$ (if all derivatives of the functions are defined)
A higher value of $n$ (order of polynomial) means a more accurate approximation
Can replace the input to these series with higher order terms / coefficients
If the input has multiple terms, do some funky stuff to get it into an addition of different Maclaurin series with single-term inputs
Don't be shy to multiply different series together
You can also expand a Maclaurin series inside another one
For these cases, ignore any operations that result in higher order term than the degree you are interested in (e.g. if 4 ignore all $x^5$ and higher)
